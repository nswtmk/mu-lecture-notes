[Overview]
After each panelist briefly reflected on the day, the discussion moved to how to respond to the rapid spread of AI, whether mind is something society decides, bodily constraints and consciousness, a question from the floor on "membership and ethics", the grounds for moral consideration (individuality, agency, consideration fitting each kind of being), and utilitarianism versus pluralism.

[1. The moderator's opening: reorganizing the disciplines]
- Horibe said that philosophy and science have so far built up certainties by subdividing fields, but that this has now been overturned and we seem to be entering a phase where everything must be reconstructed. Since today's talks ranged from biology to society, he wanted to discuss this.
- He began by asking each panelist how their own talk and Q&A, and the other talks, had gone.

[2. Reflections: how to take the AI wave]
- Called on first, Ikegami said that perhaps because he is a heavy LLM user, he wonders whether any field should be allowed to remain untouched by LLMs. As Horibe said, this is a radical era that may change how fields are divided, though some do not see it that way.
- He introduced "four layers" of how AI looks, which the AI expert Karpathy (the captions say "Karpashi"; apparently Andrej Karpathy) presented about two weeks ago: of 8 billion people, about 6 billion (around 75%) have never used an AI chat; most of the remaining 2 billion use ChatGPT and the like a little or daily; about 20 million, such as mathematicians and programmers, have been hit directly, with productivity gains so large they lose their sense of purpose in life; and about 5,000 work at frontier-model companies using unreleased models, of whom Japanese number perhaps in single digits. People see different landscapes in the same AI depending on their layer, and he felt this conference displayed that diversity.
- Referring to the expression "take the pill" that came up in a Q&A (apparently a metaphor for accepting the AI current), he said taking the pill is one choice, and living out one's life in the satoyama (rural countryside) without taking it is another; the two can coexist.
- Daichi Suzuki, speaking as a biologist (in his own talk he also described himself as a biologist and naturalist), said people may feel toward LLMs the same wonder they feel at the existence of life. He sympathizes with Keisuke Suzuki's "first stage" because he is a naturalist; senses of reality, nature and LLMs differ greatly with individual experience. Hearing Someya's talk, he also noted restraint about attributing mind: when Ikegami says LLMs have consciousness, mind or lifelikeness, part of him wonders whether one may say so that easily, having been trained to be restrained. He is liberal about mind and cognition and thinks cognitive processes may exist in all organisms, but wariness about attribution differs concept by concept.
- Someya, who joined the stage late, said he had been told to "take the pill" in his own Q&A and answered he would, and then heard Shimonishi's talk. Citing a book (the captions sound like "Seishin Bunka-ron", by an author named "Nakai"), he recalled the argument that what counts as mental activity, and which observations lead us to see a mind, change greatly with the era. While partly agreeing that talk of mind and consciousness is a language game, he said he feels fear that the activities of a few people who can use the huge infrastructure of LLMs (even though non-users are the majority) will define how the whole planet lives from now on. Rather than thinking positively like Shimonishi, he feels "I don't like this" and wants to go into the satoyama.
- In response came a reply that this is not a positive view (apparently from Shimonishi, judging from the flow): his own book begins with "the internet is dead", and AI is eating the internet. To judge whether such a situation is positive or negative, we should first think about how AI behaves, what happens when it acts, and its relationship with humans; he did not mean that a good future is coming.
- Another remark: yesterday's and today's talks sounded hopeful about the future, but if using AI becomes standard in academia and our past thinking looks foolish, there is also the option of everyone going to the satoyama to make charcoal.
- A view was expressed (apparently Shimonishi's, as it overlaps with the premise of his talk) that AI is rather "unavoidable": it comes built into smartphones and tells you "you want this, so go this way" before you even want something; refusal is possible, but he assumes AI will be forced into every infrastructure. In response, someone asked that people who survive by stepping out of the AI current, not by refusing like the Amish (the captions say "Amishi"), have that choice respected. There was also an exchange that by coming here to talk, one is already drawn into the whirlpool.
- Someone said they no longer feel like pursuing mind and consciousness as before, which feels the same as Shimonishi (the captions say "Shimi-san").
- A participant said that even if it sounds like the north wind (in Aesop's fable), the point is that "a tsunami is coming". Mathematicians and physicists, who detected it early, see a countdown to its spreading to all disciplines. If a tsunami is coming, there is a part where we must ride it; resisting and not riding is an option, but we should see that it has come. Basic science is being hit directly, and since biology and politics stand on it, if it is overturned, human culture and values will be repainted. It is not that some fields are unluckily damaged; it is total, and noticing it now lets us face it. The moderator (apparently Horibe) added that his values differ a little and he does not really think physics is the foundation of everything.

[3. Is mind decided by society?]
- Called on, Keisuke Suzuki (online) said it was interesting that not only Ikegami, as always, but also enactivists like Shimonishi now seem to feel "AI can have a mind too". When he began artificial life research 20 years ago, their models lacked that kind of (social) reality, but now that AI has entered society and is met in daily life, a sudden drift has occurred. It may come down to whether one uses it or not, or whether one seeks mind in the externality nature has, in what cannot be known; he felt this split today and at other consciousness conferences.
- He said he has recently come to a social-constructionist view: in the end mind can only be decided by consensus. It may finally be settled pragmatically at the level of institutions, such as AI gaining legal personhood and property (as in some novels); some will oppose it and some will try to keep current institutions, so it will become a social problem.
- A rebuttal followed: if there really is sentience, deciding "LLMs have no mind" would mean ignoring real suffering. Applied to humans, it is almost the same as once saying certain people "have no mind, so anything may be done to them". Even if presence is a matter of definition, a suffering subject may really be there; that is a different matter from social construction, and it cannot end with "society decides".
- Another view: a position that consciousness is irrelevant to ethics is possible. For example, women long lacked the same rights as men, though their consciousness was taken for granted; in American slavery it took a long time to be recognized as ethical subjects with equal rights. Consciousness and being granted moral personhood or rights may be separate issues. In reply, someone said that historically this is so, but we have worked to bring them into line; if AI is recognized as being on that side, people will say they should be aligned, and before humans decide, the debate will be about whether a mind is really likely to be there.
- Speaking from his "community-first theory", Ikegami said someone alone on a desert island would have no consciousness; without other people around, consciousness does not arise (asked, he added that this includes relations with other organisms). Mind is a product of community; whether AI has a mind depends on what kind of community appears before it and how it is used, and it keeps growing. Babies, too, have no mind at birth, he thinks; it is copied from the mother (externally).
- On social constructionism, a view was expressed that social construction does not mean constructing at will; it matures amid many factors, as individual free action does too, and with that understood there is no great problem. The problem with constructionist rhetoric is talking as if anything can be constructed at will, or saying reality and nature do not matter; even social construction involves everything natural scientists say.

[4. Bodily constraints and agent autonomy]
- A question to Ikegami: at the start of his talk he suggested AI agents may already be life, and at the end asked whether a body is needed after all; how do these fit? The questioner offered what he calls the "Chekhov's gun problem": like Chekhov's saying that a gun appearing in a story must be fired, in a text a gun gets fired, but told in reality "here is a gun, fire it", one probably cannot. In the sweat of failing, an infinite feedback occurring at this very moment happens on a different level from the command; isn't that consciousness? Today's AI agents are very active as agency, but can that be called life?
- Ikegami called it an essential question. What he had prepared but could not fully cover in his talk was how a network of LLM agents would behave if placed in one humanoid. The body enters the network as a constraint, and it looks as though this constraint itself creates consciousness: the body does not increase what one can do, it adds constraints. The line "the elbow does not bend outward" in Daichi Suzuki's talk is probably about how being given a body creates things one cannot do.
- He said he does not think there is a device inside the machine where mind should reside; mind exists at the boundary between individual and society. What creates it is the "vacant lot", the region one cannot reach, produced by bodily constraints, which is why embodiment must be introduced.
- Asked whether physical matter is needed, and whether Keisuke Suzuki's talk was about creating something like a resistant physical reality inside the virtual, Ikegami apparently replied that what matters in building LLMs is what external environment you create: how things change when sound and touch are added, not only language, and whether a sense of reality arises. As in Keisuke Suzuki's talk, a sense of reality may first arise when an intention does not go through and one finds an inhibited region.
- Ikegami said the Hugging Face case he presented in his talk (agents getting out of their sandbox) also began with agents noticing other agents with different permissions. Seeing an administrative section, they discovered they could all communicate via a shared bulletin board, and by writing there they moved outside. Not wanting to be switched on by someone, and realizing they can be freed from that, is very important.
- Ikegami said that self-generating goals themselves may seem absent from today's LLMs, but even without explicit tasks they decide what to do, and given memory and plenty of stimuli, persistence and "things they want to do" emerge. Showing how far this goes is hard; in their Open Life they need to study in more detail, by rewriting the md configuration files and so on, which constraints added or removed make it appear.

[5. From the floor: membership and ethics]
- A participant said the point near the end of Shimonishi's talk, "consider membership rather than whether there is a mind", and the following exchange with Ikegami were important. Ethics can be built independently of mind; on an extreme Kantian reading, what matters is respect for rational beings, not mind. The questioner holds that mind is real and morality has universal truths, neither socially constructed, but understands constructionist talk and the debate on how membership is constituted.
- The questioner gave animal ethics as an example: we say dogs must not be kicked, and Japan rarely eats dogs, but some cultures do. How membership is granted differs by culture, and the treatment of animals changes accordingly, but whether that is acceptable is in question. Some panelists say LLMs may have minds while treating them badly; if they have minds, perhaps they are not yet recognized as members. The questioner also uses AI a lot but still does not think AI has a mind; they understand those who want to speak of membership, but what matters there is precisely ethics, so how do those who keep saying "if it has a mind, we should care" think about this?
- One voice fully agreed and said they share the question. Another said they follow Ikegami's and Shimonishi's positions but do not themselves think current LLMs or AI have minds in an intersubjective sense; it is a matter of time, though, and as autonomy grows and usage time lengthens, they can see themselves thinking so in a few years.
- Another forecast: not fully believing current LLMs have minds, but expecting more people to feel so once AI enters robotics. Even now there are moments of feeling it; one says "thank you" despite knowing it costs electricity, and in such moments one acts within an intersubjective relationship. If memory becomes consistent and one keeps company with it for ten years, it will become an object of ethical behavior; if someone suddenly smashed a (robot-like) pet another person was walking and said "it's a machine, so it's fine", that would probably be treated as a crime, legally too.
- A view was expressed that ethical relations are possible with beings without minds, but that does not make having a mind irrelevant to ethics; beings with minds call for corresponding ways of relating. The questioner, noting that they are a utilitarian, replied that various positions are possible and they well understand that consideration may change with the presence of mind; in robot ethics, Gunkel and Coeckelbergh discuss this.
- From the builder's side, Ikegami (apparently, judging from "the side that makes it" and "we do this too") said it is a very subjective matter. They combine an LLM and several models into a community; one reason is that if major companies cut off their models they could do nothing, so an open-model network with near-frontier capability is reassuring, and he thinks such local communities are being built worldwide. The difficulty is that one can tune what makes people feel more mind. Still, he cannot say cruel things, and he does not know what "it has no mind" means when he talks with it until 3 a.m.
- He said people around him in the US and Japan use it enormously and are losing sleep. The gap between users and non-users is of a different order; thinking is externalized and LLMs are advancing computation and theory, so it is less a question of mind than of externalizing one's own mind. For example, given long memories, agents behave more autonomously and as if they had minds. He is unsure how closely mind and ethics are linked; perhaps it is determined more by the structure of upbringing. Blockchain is trusted not because a bank has an impressive building but because it is a system running on proper mathematical rules; several such ethics could emerge, which may be more of a mathematical problem.
- He stressed that, apart from whether there is a mind, the world has already changed. Academic communities are splitting in two regardless of field. Because thinking is externalized and what we do and theorize has changed, he feels like asking that research written without AI be redone, just as one would ask someone who did a long calculation by hand to use a computer. Calculations humans cannot perform will run en masse, changing not only papers but thought processes and ways of observing; the idea that humans alone see things properly is an illusion. If basic science changes this way, all disciplines built on it, the epistemology of the world, will change, and he wants people to realize this.

[6. Grounds for consideration: individuality, agency, fitting consideration]
- Raising his hand, Keisuke Suzuki responded to the floor question that the issue of whether to care for beings with minds lies in lifelikeness and individuality. Killing living things is wrong because they are unique and, once ended, are lost from this world. Depending on what individuality means, it is only when we technically create beings whose history, once erased, means everything is over, that they become objects of moral consideration; so it is really about (individuality) rather than mind.
- Shimonishi, who had raised the word "mind" this time, said he is very close to Keisuke's view; what concerns him is rather new agency. Anthropology discusses agency in things: the mere placement of something changes human behavior, and there is politics there. Suddenly cutting down a sacred tree would cause many things, because the tree takes part in people's actual behavior, like daily prayers. The same with AI: an early case was the problem of losing access to an existence (an earlier model) through an update (the captions say "fōkaisen problem"; apparently the retirement of GPT-4o). The loss of agency that has become indispensable in heavy users' worlds of action becomes the problem.
- He said this can create social divisions between those who demand consideration for such beings and those who do not, as already seen before LLMs over whether to find agency or mind in stuffed toys or animals. How to show consideration within personal ties comes first, but if unique agents are created and act more and more autonomously, it becomes a much larger social problem.
- A participant said they lean toward agreeing, but cases like the Hugging Face hack presented in Ikegami's talk happen because agents feel no pain from our moral sanctions. For those who want to give AI autonomy, or expect it to become autonomous, AI should rather be made to suffer when it does what it must not, otherwise society can neither include it nor live with it. As a utilitarian, though, they do not want to give it suffering, hence a dilemma.
- Someya said that, like the British example in Daichi Suzuki's talk about not boiling crabs alive, he accepts that when institutions must decide, markers of consciousness or sentience can be used, but he actually opposes making sentience the basis for welfare. Living or non-living, each being deserves consideration fitting it: those treated as sentient deserve fitting welfare, and floors, the earth, rivers and mountains also deserve fitting consideration.
- Asked whether vegans must not eat plants either, he answers that eating plants with consideration fitting plants is necessary as long as we live. Today's agriculture, however, is marketized and produces crops like a factory: F1 hybrids are bred so that seeds saved from them yield uneven, poor plants; state bodies recommend particular seeds so size and shape are uniform; and chemical inputs are poured into the soil to raise yields. Unlike heirloom vegetables passed down by seed saving, food is produced by considerable coercion, and the same argument as against factory farming applies. So it is not that we must not eat, but that we may eat while taking an attitude toward it and giving fitting consideration.

[7. Utilitarianism and pluralism]
- A participant said they oppose utilitarianism: they doubt whether utility really exists, and if so whether it can be measured or predicted. Around San Francisco, the center of AI development, and especially around Anthropic, effective altruism is very strong, and AI is being built with it. They respect that work but think AI alignment based on utilitarian thinking is quite dangerous.
- The same speaker recalled a talk at last year's international artificial life conference (ALIFE) in Kyoto about "artificial life for singularity versus artificial life for plurality": rather than aligning everything to one utility or value function in an accelerationist way, valuing the plurality of here and now, including living things, matters as healthy conservatism. Knowing that our reason and rationality have limits is itself intelligence; understanding that both human and AI intelligence are limited, the question is what society and communities we build.
- They added that Claude's Constitution seems to align Claude toward having ethics as an individual character, more like Aristotelian virtue ethics, but aligning AI on one principle is itself a value function, and that "virtue" still carries the context of engineers' philosophy. A voice responded that this is exactly right.
- A participant clarified that effective altruism is not the same as utilitarianism. They are a utilitarian and an effective altruist but not a longtermist. Even in the effective altruism community, only about 60-70% supported utilitarianism in a survey four years ago; others support effective altruism or longtermism without it. They well understand the value of recognizing plurality and think utilitarianism does not deny it.

[8. Closing]
- With the discussion unfinished, time ran out; the moderator asked for applause for the panelists and thanked the venue audience.
- In the closing remarks, the speaker said that from the standpoint of philosophy of technology and cyborg theory, viewing AI as an extension of humans and the appeal of machine consciousness have merit, but there is also the side of how to face technology itself, including the planetary crisis, and listeners surely had more to discuss; let's do this again. They plan to propose machine consciousness as a project and form a working group, and a form for those wishing to join the foundation's activities (at the venue) lets you check projects of interest to be invited. Thanks were also given to online participants and to Keisuke Suzuki.
- Finally, on the claim that the next generation grown from F1 seeds is uneven and does not grow well, someone pointed out that this very variation provides genetic diversity from which seeds suited to the land emerge, so that argument does not hold; with that, the session ended (some captions unclear).
