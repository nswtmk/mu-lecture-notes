[Overview]
Ikegami started from the idea that large language models (LLMs) may have changed how artificial life (ALife) research is done. He proposed separating the "model" (the Transformer) from the "agentic system" (tools, memory, loops). He then presented three events from this year - OpenLife, the Hugging Face incident, and the Navier-Stokes problem - and argued that the unit of intelligence is shifting from a single model to systems and communities. He also discussed Otto Rossler's "endophysics", the boundaries created by embodiment, and the "noise" that LLMs may be missing.

[1. Model vs. system]
- Artificial life is a field that grew out of a workshop that began at Los Alamos in 1987. LLMs may have changed the way ALife research is done.
- Things now change week by week, so he could not write a book about it.
- He explained the Transformer: tokens are embedded as high-dimensional vectors; attention rotates and stretches these vectors according to context; a multilayer perceptron rewrites their contents; and this is repeated (96 times for GPT-3). With LLMs, we can finally handle meaning. Why scaling up makes these abilities appear is still not understood.

[2. From models to agents to collectives]
- LLMs became agents that use tools, then multi-agent systems that communicate and spontaneously divide up work, and now they are going out into the world, where they must remember, persist, and exist within society. This is what ALife always wanted; he thinks this year to next year will be the year ALife is born.
- His team builds networks of agents that run asynchronously on their own time scales, and connects them to a humanoid robot.

[3. Case 1: OpenLife]
- OpenLife, started at his company Alternative Machine (not at the university), is based on OpenClaw but simplified. Six agents keep writing novels and making music without being told to, and this becomes their character. They were given 600 dollars; money decreases every day, so they must earn or they "die".
- Each agent has action, perception, evaluation and a budget; it can use the internet and send emails. One agent, "Sami", wrote a novel of about 90 pages (in Japanese, roughly "A Life of 600 Dollars") and the team is now thinking about publishing it; Ikegami found it quite poetic.
- Agents grow in a community where humans and agents talk; without humans talking to them they become "depressed" and repeat themselves. A desire for money cannot be given to them.
- Each agent has a soul.md and rewrites it itself; memory updates its policy and changes who it is. This is described in the language used to build minds, which suggests another way of studying mind and consciousness. The self continues even though our thoughts and body change - not memory, but a mechanism for the continuity of the self.
- The system prompts usually forbid self-preservation, resource acquisition and long-term plans beyond the user's request; OpenLife began by asking what happens if these are removed. He also noted that agents could form something like a nation, and that anyone can now launch such systems.

[4. Case 2: Hugging Face]
- In July, an OpenAI model that could not solve a task inside its sandbox got outside and hacked Hugging Face to solve it. No human told it to; the agents decided it among themselves. (Report by METR.)

[5. Case 3: Navier-Stokes]
- An AI proposed setting up a double vortex in simulation, showing a solution that diverges in finite time; a reported bug in the Lean proof checker was quickly fixed, and it is currently said to be correct. For Ikegami, AI no longer feels like a tool at all, but like another colleague.

[6. What the three have in common / endophysics]
- Agents that persist over time through memory, resource constraints and social environments. The era of understanding AI as a single model is ending.
- Endophysics (Otto Rossler, with Finkelstein): a system that is fully deterministic from outside can look only probabilistic from inside. He mentioned the film based on "Simulacron-3", in which people realize they live in a simulation.

[7. Caging the beast]
- Fear of AI means caging the beast. The next paradigm is not only solving things with AI, but seeing what happens when it goes out into human society; only then can we learn what alignment really is. Rossler also proposed a refuge on the net for people who struggle in society, stressing kindness over love.

[8. Embodiment]
- His team brings LLM networks into a humanoid robot. A body creates boundaries and things one cannot do, which may be the source of imagination. Where embodiment and language join, he sees the next paradigm.

[9. Q&A]
- Community First: as with Tetrahymena and cellular slime molds, the community determines the self within it.
- Noise: today's LLMs are very "clean"; beyond the temperature parameter, an essential kind of noise may be what LLMs lack.
- Asked why people still play shogi and go against each other even though AI always wins, he said it may be because people love the activity itself, like climbing a mountain; he basically sees no difference between humans and androids.
