[Overview]
Suzuki, who has worked on VR research that alters the sense of self and on the "Hallucination Machine", which reproduces hallucinatory experience with deep neural networks, said that to think about consciousness he wanted, conversely, to think about "reality". As preliminaries, he noted that the controversy over Integrated Information Theory (IIT) is still unresolved; that both the free energy principle and IIT are built on existing mathematics and technology with philosophical interpretation added; and that recent models of mind are concerned with where to draw the boundary between inside and outside. He then defined reality as "resistance to our workings on the world" and argued that reality is one, but appears in three aspects depending on how it resists: "substrate", "contingency" and "inevitability". He named structures designed to resist even their own makers' intervention - such as smart contracts on a blockchain - "artificial externality", argued that how we grasp reality shapes how we grasp consciousness, and presented an implementation of an agent with "cryptographic individuality", whose neural network weights are derived from a secret key. The Q&A discussed failure and the sense of reality, what changes by introducing cryptographic keys, how to conduct debates on consciousness, the closure of subjectivity and the outside, and individuality and plant consciousness.

[1. Introduction: thinking about reality in order to think about consciousness]
- Suzuki joined online; he had just attended an international consciousness conference and was staying in the UK for a while.
- He said he would give "a slightly crazy talk", different from his usual work, under the title "artificial externality".
- He has worked on VR research, including changing the sense of self with VR, and more recently on the "Hallucination Machine", which reproduces hallucinatory experience with deep neural networks; the problem of consciousness is his core interest.
- In that line, he had long thought that to think about consciousness it might be better to deliberately think about reality instead.

[2. Preliminary 1: the debate over consciousness theories is still unresolved]
- The Templeton Foundation has since around 2021 funded "adversarial collaborations" to settle by experiment whether IIT or rivals such as predictive processing or global workspace theory are right (IIT vs predictive processing, IIT vs global workspace, and others ongoing), with results coming out or not.
- Around then, an open letter appeared in the ASSC circle and caused trouble: 124 people signed regarding IIT, and IIT supporters and opponents split clearly. According to the slide, the letter (September 2023) called IIT "pseudoscience".
- Finally in 2025, Nature Neuroscience published three papers - from the IIT side, the anti-IIT side, and an arbitrating one by Anil Seth - settling it at the level of commentary, but which theory is correct is of course not settled. Results are still awaited, and apparently neural representationalism vs IIT has now started too.
- He does not think the ASSC-circle theories are simply right; after Someya's and Ikegami's talks, consciousness should be grasped more broadly, but this is the state of neuroscientific consciousness research.

[3. Preliminary 2: technology and models of mind]
- Both the free energy principle (an extension of the theory behind predictive processing) and IIT seem to be preceded by certain theories or technologies.
- For example, the free energy principle relies on Conant and Ashby's "good regulator" theorem - a controller should be a model of what it controls - combined (according to the slide, with variational Bayesian inference) so that a system beginning to infer the world is identified with cognition. A foundational mathematics or theory exists, and philosophical interpretation is added.
- IIT uses a new information theory looking at integration and differences of information, but much cannot be explained by mathematics alone, so interpretation enters; especially strong is the claim that having causal power equals existence. Attaching such strong ontological commitments to existing theories produces theories of consciousness.
- This is probably not new (it overlaps with a book by Shimonishi): the mind is a model. Machines that project the world (cameras); telephone and telegraph networks leading to nerves as wires; then cybernetics, digital control and digital computers - control engineering and computers came first.
- Transformers and current AI will likely enter this picture; technology and models of mind interact, though not one-to-one. The reverse also happens: brain models and theoretical hypotheses produced neural networks and logic design, so the direction is not always from technology to mind.

[4. Preliminary 3: where to draw the boundary between inside and outside]
- Following Someya's talk, he said that since the 20th century the question has become where to draw the line of consciousness and mind. Andy Clark's extended mind, enactivism, the free energy principle and IIT each draw the boundary differently; Markov blankets and the operational closure in enactivism are not consciousness itself, but there is still a tendency to divide inside and outside.
- Conversely, some currents say we should not divide, or that there is no such boundary - for example non-dual consciousness without self-other separation in Zen meditation.
- But the broad trend in recent models of mind is to divide inside from outside and to conceive of mind as an inside facing an outside. This is not all of Western thought, but within Western values that treat humans as special, where to draw this line relates to how to cross the gap. Today he would talk about this "outside".

[5. Reality as "resistance to our workings on the world"]
- Apologizing for slides that look very generative-AI-like (he just wanted to try it once), he recalled the 18th-century anecdote of kicking a stone to refute idealism, "I refute it thus" (according to the slide, Samuel Johnson against Berkeley's idealism, 1763): the pain in the foot, the feel of the stone at the toe.
- Physical resistance - the world is there because we can touch it - was, if not the only, the most common rebuttal and claim.
- Now AI quality is rising, and crypto-assets, central to today's talk: one can call money itself an illusion, but today's crypto-assets have become almost an independent system, outside states.
- Definition: there are various workings on the world, and when different forms of resistance come back in response, we take that resistance as reality - reality is given that way.
- Simply put, three: "substrate", the reality of physical silicon, energy and heat; the reality of experience, which cannot be doubted - the sense that reality presses upon us; and "inevitability", reality appearing as resistance to attempts to control it.
- He is not dividing reality into three; there is only one reality, but its appearance differs by the mode of resistance, so it can be cut along three aspects.

[6. Layer 1, substrate: matter cannot be bypassed]
- Layers 1 and 2 are not central, so he went quickly. Speculative realism arose as a critique of what it calls correlationism (e.g., phenomenology) - the world being determined by the mind.
- They (Meillassoux, per the slide) point to (isotope) half-lives and light from stars - things existing before humans - so we must think a world independent of human thought exists. Harman says objects in the physical world "withdraw": they can never be exhausted relationally, having infinitely many relations beyond what we model or describe. Reality lies in that inexhaustibility.
- For information there is Landauer's theorem (the slide: information is physical; erasing information always generates heat).
- The person who developed Mathematica (Wolfram, per the slide) says that for some things, not all, you cannot know the result without actually running it; information is not just held - computation may have to happen in the real world.
- So, as an anticipated rebuttal to what follows, computation inevitably rides on the physical layer; in that sense substrate matters. He would later connect this to biological naturalism.

[7. Layer 2, contingency: "exists" versus "this is real"]
- Floridi says that once physical laws are made into laws, the informational structure is what is real - but he does not reduce things to information.
- Complementary to this, Chalmers says that things made of data structures and algorithms, if they appear as objects and interact through the subject, are real objects.
- But at this point we can say they "exist", which is different from "this is reality".
- Husserl's "Urdoxa": an undeniable belief (or perhaps not a belief) that reality is there - the sense that what we now experience cannot be doubted.
- At RIKEN he built the Substitutional Reality (SR) system: a camera on a head-mounted display, plus past footage (per the slide, from a 360-degree panoramic camera).
- Ordinary VR can feel very real, but only "as if"; it never reaches the belief that it is reality. SR tried to go beyond that: instead of pursuing image quality, it lowered the quality, making people believe past footage was happening here and now.
- He showed an old video switching from live to past footage; switching while the head moves causes blur, so it is hardly noticed. Most people realize it is not real when they see themselves appear in the past footage, but such VR mechanisms can be built - this is the second layer.

[8. Layer 3, inevitability: "artificial externality" that binds even its makers]
- Third, quite different: not reality but facts - Searle's "institutional facts". Money, borders and property are a kind of shared illusion that holds because everyone more or less accepts it, and it can collapse.
- Meanwhile, blockchains such as Bitcoin now fully support smart contracts, programs executed automatically on the chain, enabling decentralized exchanges and much else. Code without humans: no one presses a start button; when conditions are met it runs automatically, and it can be programmed so no one can tamper with it - the key to decentralization.
- The program runs not on one machine but on nodes worldwide; destroying one computer does not stop it. As long as consensus holds and people participate as nodes, it cannot be stopped by its maker alone.
- He thought this a new type of externality and named it "artificial externality": structures designed that way. Nuclear waste looks similar but was not designed so, so it differs.
- He mentioned the Capek brothers' "Adam the Creator" (written after R.U.R.), in which Adam builds a "negation cannon" able to destroy the world and is troubled by how to handle it. Structures hard to dismantle or deal with exist, but blockchains and smart contracts differ in that they can now be designed and actually run in the real world.
- This externality differs from what Kant, Latour or Harman described: it is engineering-oriented, an externality of non-intervenability that can be made. With reservations: it is autonomous only from the maker's intention, depends on electricity and validators, and has holes such as 51% attacks. As long as it works, it has acquired a new externality.

[9. How we grasp reality shapes how we grasp consciousness]
- Connecting to consciousness (also in his paper): with these three layers (not only three), theories and views of consciousness relate to how one grasps reality.
- Those emphasizing substrate take biological naturalism, as Anil Seth (whom he had met the day before) does, holding that AI in principle cannot be conscious. Suzuki finds it puzzling why Anil values substrate so much: life could reside in processes or dynamics, yet Anil insists on matter shaped by biological evolution (not necessarily neural). Perhaps he has in mind matter and something like Harman's inexhaustibility.
- Valuing contingency depends on whether one grants reality to information structures, algorithms or software, not fully detached from physical bases. Suzuki, originally from physics and later artificial life, said something to the effect that he feels a strong sense of reality in computation (the captions are fragmentary).
- Third, some think of consciousness as social-cognitive construction: whether something is conscious is decided by whether others recognize it. Not mainstream, but he finds it important, especially given anthropomorphic views of AI.
- No single stance is most important; each layer has its own reality, and the question is which to value.

[10. Agency and implementing "cryptographic individuality"]
- Separate from but parallel to consciousness, agency - purposive, normative action - is important. Barandiaran and colleagues give three conditions: individuality, interactional asymmetry and normativity. Individuality: making a membrane, being separated from the environment; asymmetry: action; normativity: deciding good and bad.
- They discuss biological agency: individuality as self-maintenance through producing (a membrane) (per the slide, "self-maintenance through metabolism"); asymmetry as cells moving, having a body that moves around.
- Adding the third-layer idea, he has been thinking that maintaining a cryptographic identity might yield cryptographic individuality. Asymmetry and normativity lie outside this, but he tried first building individuality on a new reality.
- Shifting abruptly to blockchain technology, he said he actually built it: neural network weights are derived from a secret key, held by the host and not published on chain, so others cannot know the weights.
- Each step's neural network update is done not on chain but by a node operator, using zero-knowledge proofs to prove the correctness of the computation.
- The benefit: for now the weights cannot change much, but it is guaranteed that the weights W come from a secret key only the agent holds and are not disclosed on chain, while computations based on W are certainly performed.
- As an extension, the agent has a wallet and cannot update without a balance - quite close to what Ikegami and Masumori do (he had not heard Ikegami's talk).
- Not yet fully working, but (homeostatic regulation) has been added; external input/output is implemented but not connected to the network. It is a tiny three-layer neural network, with all code on GitHub.

[11. Technology changing models of mind again]
- Returning to the start: LLMs will become models of mind, but afterwards, in deciding the boundary of the mind and what counts as a responsible subject, once blockchains make possible a cryptographically and mathematically guaranteed self, an era may come when technology again changes models of mind. He is implementing ahead of that.
- Inevitability can now be engineered with smart contracts; whether an agent built cryptographically is autonomous in a strong sense depends on what one considers real. His own remarks continued in the direction of seeing it as autonomous in a strong sense (the captions break off; the slide's take-home message says it "may have autonomy in a strong sense").
- This also becomes an ethical issue; ultimately, if a social consensus arises that AI consciousness or rights must be recognized, so be it. Still, a (cryptographically) guaranteed individual is something today's LLMs entirely lack, and he thinks new insight may lie there.
- The slide lists the paper "Artificial Externality: A Three-Layer Model of Reality from Substrate to Smart Contract" (Philosophy and Technology, 2026) and "Internalising the Identity Primitive" (arXiv) on the implementation.

[12. Q&A]
- A participant mentioned a recent incident involving Hugging Face, asking whether "they" had a sense of reality while constrained and when they suddenly escaped through a hole; and, citing a philosopher (apparently Heidegger) on consciousness arising when we fail, suggested that reality appears when, e.g., a robot does not move as intended and meets constraints - where does this fit among layers 1-3? Suzuki said it sounds like layer 3, but consciousness and reality seem mixed up.
- When the participant added that we feel reality when we fail, Suzuki agreed that we do feel reality in failure, and that this may apply to all layers: since reality is resistance to our workings, if things go smoothly, confirmation of reality does not arise; when resistance comes back as failure we feel reality - perhaps the third. The first is epistemological - what inevitably slips through when we try to know (maybe an illusion). Whether reality differs qualitatively is unclear, but though people's notions of reality differ slightly, all may be resistance.
- A participant said they did not fully understand agency via cryptographic keys: an LLM given memory may behave like an agent with individuality, so what changes by introducing keys? Suzuki said perhaps only our human way of seeing changes; cryptographic guarantees do not increase the agent's abilities. Rather, something changes when we, or an AI agent with some self-model, realize that it is autonomous.
- He added that today's LLMs are experimented on from inside by companies - (per the captions) finding a "pain" vector and increasing or decreasing it - which would be ethically impermissible if they were conscious. Human rights are protected partly because one cannot physically poke electrodes into our brains from outside; what he calls individuality includes something like privacy protection. Only when internal updates, including updates of W, are not merely untouchable but invisible from outside might they - and we humans - become safe in a sense.
- A participant agreed with the slide that how we grasp reality shapes how we grasp consciousness, and said debate cannot progress without sharing views of reality; speakers each hold different views and try to convey them by armoring themselves with science, philosophy, papers and experiments - a roundabout approach. Instead, e.g., playing together with the agent Ikegami made, or talking night after night with the AI Kanai is raising, might be needed for alignment. Where are the problems in current debates and activities, and how to overcome them? Suzuki said this becomes very important when looking at the IIT debate: critics notably criticize without understanding the theory, and the public is even more confused. So he wrote the philosophy paper and simultaneously implemented and ran it; otherwise it ends up as "just scholars arguing". He hopes the Consciousness Research Foundation can connect both: touching plants and living in satoyama, and implementing consciousness from the technical side. The participant replied that even among scholars, armoring and new papers do not seem to resolve the gaps, so it may be important for everyone, scholars included, to do such things.
- A participant, interested in the inside/outside boundary discussion (forcibly divided, e.g., by Markov blankets), asked for confirmation: subjectivity is closed in autopoietic and other cognitive-science senses, somewhat solipsistic, with even criteria of judgment subjective; in that sense the outside is posited as other than oneself - is that different from Suzuki's sense? Suzuki said it may be related but differs from his talk: dividing inside and outside, the inside does feel real, but he considers reality including that inside feeling real; what was described probably corresponds to the second layer. The participant said it was good to confirm the difference.
- A participant (using the word "real existence" for reality) said they sympathized with reality as resistance and with individuality being important for consciousness, but asked: in plants, individuality is weaker than in animals; if consciousness requires individuality, is plant consciousness hard to grant, and do Someya and Suzuki conflict? Suzuki said he feels closer to Someya: perhaps we just cannot recognize consciousness without individuality; consciousness riding on individual-like things is one element, and if other forms are possible, plants may well be conscious. Likewise, unless time scales are similar we cannot even judge whether conscious phenomena occur, so it may be fine to broaden the notion.
- He added that consciousness researchers want to use the term more narrowly, but there is a spectrum: even at neuroscience conferences, among attention and metacognition researchers, views vary widely on whether a seeing self (agent) is assumed behind. So there is no real fight; he understands Someya well.
- The participant then offered a rebuttal themselves: even if individuality is not an absolutely necessary condition for consciousness in general, could being an individual be indispensable for something's having consciousness? Suzuki said it is possible; IIT proponents say something close: not all phenomena are conscious, and if having an integrated informational state (they do not call it an individual) and a sense of unity matter, it may be required. But it depends on how far one grants consciousness; he thinks unintegrated consciousness could exist, so it becomes a definitional problem.
- Asked whether unintegrated cases then involve another type of consciousness, Suzuki said different modes of consciousness may exist, though if some functions appear only with integration, he understands wanting to call that consciousness. The participant said that a view granting only that would deny consciousness to plants and to meditative states of oneness with the universe, and asked whether Suzuki does not hold such a view; Suzuki replied, "That's right." Time ran out there.
