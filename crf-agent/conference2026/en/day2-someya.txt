[Overview]
Someya, who has worked in ecological psychology in the Gibsonian tradition, asked: "Organisms without a stomach can still digest, so why do we assume that without a brain or nerves there can be no consciousness or workings of the mind?" He introduced research on perception and behavior in organisms without nervous systems, especially plants: the birth of plant neurobiology and the controversy around it; the "chameleon plant controversy" over the vine Boquila, whose leaves come to resemble even plastic leaves; an experiment in which pea tendrils change their movement before touching a support; and the cellular basis of consciousness hypothesis and basal cognition. He described these as a movement to make living and the workings of the mind continuous or identical, which he calls "animacy psychology". Locating the essence of the mind in sensing one's surroundings from one's own point of view and choosing and changing one's relation to the environment (the "ecological character" of the mind), he said that in AI and robots he cannot see this ecological character, nor life (being alive), nor the valuation of the surroundings needed to keep living (the three being restatements of the same thing). He then answered five expected objections. The Q&A discussed conditions for AI, whether soil is alive, plant self-identity, time scales, and the combination problem.

[1. The question: can the mind work without nerves?]
- Someya said that he has recently been practicing cutting trees and making firewood and charcoal, and that as his involvement with plants grew longer, the debates became very interesting to him.
- His talk would go further and more radically than Suzuki's, toward the consciousness and mental workings of organisms without nervous systems. Such ideas have flourished over the past 15 years or so, under names like basal cognition and plant neurobiology, studying the intelligent, mindful, conscious-like behavior of organisms without nerves. Today he would limit himself to plants.
- He originally worked in ecological psychology in the lineage of the Gibsonians, applying it to the philosophy of action and perception.
- Starting question: an amoeba, with no stomach or intestines, takes in food and digests and absorbs it; biology does not say it cannot digest because it has no stomach. Then can plants, fungi, protists and bacteria, which have no brain or nervous system, not be conscious or exercise mental workings? People often assume they cannot; he would argue against that.
- He distinguishes "cognition" from "workings of the mind": "cognition" tends to emphasize the epistemic side, so by "workings of the mind" he means something broader that includes feelings and emotions.
- This argument can go toward the view that AI-like things also have consciousness and mental workings, or toward the view that being alive is quite important and toward asking what the essence of living is.

[2. Questioning the premises of mind and consciousness]
- Why does a logic that works for the stomach and gut not work for the brain and nerves? However much psychologists and cognitive scientists say Descartes was wrong, behind this lies the idea that mind and consciousness are mental processes, digestion and absorption are physiological processes, and there is an essential difference in kind between them. He regards breaking this down as his last work.
- "Animacy psychology" comes down to this: the process of living and the presence of mental workings cannot be separated. This is what he is now exploring.
- Aim: discussing consciousness and mental workings is now in fashion, using the brain and nerves, or the human-like linguistic responses of AI, as if they were evidence of consciousness or mental workings. By contrast, arguments that plants have a mind hardly get a hearing; he feels exclusion and silence are imposed.
- He wants to resist this carefully and modestly (modestly because he cannot talk like a machine gun as Mogi does, he added). Having worked in psychology, he wants to widen or change the viewpoints from which we think about consciousness and mind, which he thinks must also change because of current AI research including LLMs.

[3. Outline]
- First, empirical research on the mental workings and consciousness of "nerveless" organisms (he keeps using this word because it is amusing, not in the sense of being insensitive). He would present plants and skip fungi and bacteria. If time allowed, he would also introduce the cellular basis of consciousness hypothesis, which holds that consciousness already exists in each cell and that the birth of life and the birth of consciousness are the same; this is currently the most radical idea.
- Second, the extension of mind and consciousness and animacy psychology. "Animacy psychology" is his own term; the idea is expressed in various ways, such as life-mind continuity, as one movement of thought making mind and life continuous.
- Third, an ecological approach to machine consciousness (what attitude we can take toward AI). Fourth, answers to objections he expects.

[4. Plant neurobiology]
- Starting with Stefano Mancuso of Italy (whose popular science books are available in Japanese translation), Baluska and others, a society bearing the name "plant neurobiology" was founded (2005, according to the slide).
- For about two years afterwards, there was a fierce controversy, almost like mutual abuse, between eminent plant physiologists and plant neurobiologists, including through exchanges of essays.
- Plants have no neurons, but the program claimed that they have bodily mechanisms that function like neurons, and was born as a program to study plant behavior actively in psychological terms. The papers of this society, beginning with the first collection "Communication in Plants", are all available online.
- Later, by a vote of its members, the society was renamed the "Society for Plant Signaling and Behavior", deciding not to use the word neurobiology. Still, Mancuso and the other founding figures continue to use it.
- It was known that action potentials run through plants, but not how they affect growth regulation. The group took this up actively and set out to study the adaptive functions of the plant body as a whole; it uses the word "neuro" strategically.

[5. Bioelectric signaling in plants]
- As an example featured in the plant episode of the NHK program "Super Evolution" (2022, according to the slide), he introduced Arabidopsis: when a leaf is eaten by an insect, calcium-ion signaling reaches other leaves, which then produce substances that cause indigestion in the insect. The plant not only produces these within itself but also releases volatile substances toward neighboring plants of the same species.
- There is also research on associative learning in peas, but he skipped it because it is disputed.

[6. The chameleon plant controversy]
- He presented the biggest recent controversy, the "chameleon plant controversy". Boquila, a vine living in South America, climbs by twining around host plants, and its leaves come to resemble (mimic) those of the host. Although it is the same species, its leaves actually come to look like the leaves of whatever host it climbs. This had long been known, but why remained unknown.
- The authors of the paper reported in 2014 proposed, without testing them, hypothesis 1 (it receives chemicals emitted by the host plant, which make its leaf shapes similar) and hypothesis 2 (host genes are transferred horizontally, or taken in via viruses or the like). At the time they said the second was most likely.
- In 2021 the authors published again, suggesting that microbes were attached (hypothesis 3). Even before that, plant neurobiologists had proposed a "vision hypothesis" (hypothesis 4), though not in a formal paper.
- Then in 2022 White and Yamashita published a paper that drew a lot of attention and became sensational. In review, they reportedly got nitpicking along the lines of "since it comes from plant neurobiology, it must be sloppy".
- The vision hypothesis holds that leaves have something like eyespots and use them to imitate.

[7. Resembling even plastic leaves?]
- The 2022 experiment was simple, like a classic comparative experiment in psychology. The host was replaced with an artificial plant with plastic leaves instead of a living plant, so that volatiles, horizontal gene transfer and bacterial communities could not be passed on.
- The leaves did come to resemble them. Plastic leaves were presented in the upper part and not in the lower part, and resemblance was judged where the vine twined. The orientation was varied (east, west, south, north) to see whether the same result appeared under different lighting. They said this could support the vision hypothesis.
- But various criticisms were raised, such as that not all confounding factors had been removed.
- Chlamydomonas, which also came up in Suzuki's talk, has a light-sensing "eyespot", and the idea is that plant leaves might have something similar. Another paper (2023) argued that "eyes" are diverse, especially among organisms without nervous systems, and that roots too have light-sensitive cells, which may relate to roots avoiding light and growing into the soil.
- The Scientist covered it as an article in 2022. One might bring Boquila to Europe to study it, but apparently it changes during air transport, so research can only be done on site; he does not know how far verification has since progressed.
- What is interesting, he said, is that the researchers do not deny from the outset that plants perceive, behave and control their behavior, which is why they design such experiments. Gagliano, who did the associative learning (classical conditioning) experiment on peas mentioned earlier, also originally belonged to plant neurobiology. Research on intelligent plant behavior had been known, but there was no context in which people actively built experiments with psychological concepts and talked about them openly. That is what is new.

[8. Do pea tendrils "know" the support?]
- In the same current, he introduced a classic experiment published in another journal on how a climbing plant regulates the growth movements by which it twines around a host. The seedling is surrounded with black and lit from one place.
- The conditions were: no pole, a real pole, and a "2D stimulus" in which a photo of a pole is put on the wall. The idea of showing a plant a photo of a pole and examining its growth had, at least until then, not existed.
- The movement was filmed continuously with a fixed camera, and kinematic properties such as acceleration, the angle and maximum distance of opening, and mean speed were measured. The movement is only about 1-2 mm per minute and looks motionless to the naked eye.
- There was no statistically significant difference between the no-pole and photo conditions; a clear difference appeared only with a real pole. Interestingly, the circumnutation became larger from a certain point before touching the pole.
- Why? Hypotheses include vision (using light information to control movement), the uptake of volatile chemicals, and an "echolocation hypothesis" in which faint sounds made during circumnutation bounce back and tell the plant a pole is there. None has yet been verified.

[9. Plant consciousness: empirical support from behavioral observation]
- Since around 2020, researchers studying plant cognition have begun to speak about plant consciousness. They claim that plants have neuron-like mechanisms and that plants do not move randomly but selectively and purposively according to their surroundings.
- Cited empirical support includes: anesthesia stopping the workings of action potentials or inducing temporary dormancy; stress responses to environmental conditions; and (partly hypothetical) because plants cannot move, they sense a huge number of external parameters - not only light but water, dryness, gravity - and regulate the whole, deciding whether to invest energy in growing roots or shoots.

[10. Cellular consciousness and basal cognition]
- Extending this, since around the 2020s the "cellular basis of consciousness (CBC) model" has appeared, which, against the idea of a neural basis of consciousness, holds that cells already have consciousness (he skipped the details).
- Their point is expressed in the preface's phrase that life and sentience are coterminous: we should ask how cells have sentience and stop asking whether brains, neurons or neural networks have minds.
- A similar idea, not directly linked to plant neurobiology but in the same current, is "basal cognition". In Japanese it was first introduced in the August issue of Nikkei Science, including an interview with Michael Levin.
- As far as he can see, the basal cognition side makes no statements about consciousness. But it does not locate the minimal unit of cognition in having anatomical neurons; it declares that the processes carried out to continue the process of living (life processes, living processes) are what it calls cognition.

[11. Summary: animacy psychology]
- In sum, there is now a movement to deliberately extend the scope of mental workings and consciousness.
- Mental workings mean being aware of, perceiving and sensing the state of one's surroundings, and changing the relation between oneself and the environment through behavior, thereby living in a way that contributes to survival. A movement to make living and mental workings continuous or identical is under way, though it has not been much featured in Japan.
- In it, the process of living, the process of mental workings and the process of consciousness (and probably cognitive and conscious processes) are not clearly distinguished, and thinking that identifies or makes all of them continuous is emerging.
- Someya reported this in 2024 as "animacy psychology" in a book on the history of the philosophy of mind. "Animacy" comes from "anima", meaning soul or being alive. He wrote that such a psychology may be possible, and that psychology departments should seriously do plant psychology.

[12. The ecological character of the mind]
- From his involvement in ecological psychology, he rephrased the continuity of living and mental workings. Mental workings here mean perception and action. Prefacing it by saying it had come up in Watanabe's talk the day before, he noted that in Nagel's "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" the term "point of view" appears again and again in rephrasing what it is to be conscious.
- Being aware (perceptually noticing) of one's surroundings here and now from one's own point of view, and choosing and changing one's relation to them through action: at least living things cannot get the food they need for survival without this. This applies to plants too, and he calls it the ecological character of mental workings.
- In Gibson's ecological psychology, affordance is famous, but Someya himself was most drawn to the earlier "visual ego" theory and theory of self-perception: being able to perceive one's surroundings is at the same time perceiving oneself looking at them. This led to affordance theory.
- As an image he showed Mach's self-portrait (the view from the left eye). The room and one's own feet are visible, and where one sits and which way one faces are perceived together with the scenery. Turning the head to the right hides what was on the left and reveals what was hidden on the right; changes in how the scene looks tell us our current posture and direction. This is discussed as the co-perception of environment and self.
- This is an example from human vision, but living beings likewise each have their own perspective in the environment, and adjust their next movement amid the huge number of environmental parameters that change as that perspective moves.
- In short, he thinks that having a point of view from which one can see and sense the world as one's own concern is the essence of having consciousness and mental workings.

[13. An ecological approach to machine consciousness]
- Saying it was a bit scary but needed saying, he listed his dissatisfactions with AI and robots (physical AI).
- He first admitted that he has only used generative AI to find typos in his own texts, so he grants that he does not interact with AI and that this is partly why he cannot think it is conscious.
- First, the absence of ecological character: AI cannot perceive and act so as to choose and change its relation to the environment as its own concern. Even if in verbal exchanges it seems to have a self, it cannot move in a real environment, use target objects or take them into its body, and so carry on the process of living. Not seeing mental workings exercised in relation to surrounding nature is why he cannot think it has consciousness or mental workings.
- Second, the absence of life: in other words, it is not alive. But AI is not dead either. It is neither a living thing nor a non-living thing, but a puzzling, alien-like existence.
- Third, the absence of valuation of the surroundings for continuing to live in relation to them - even the most primitive judgment of whether something is harmful or beneficial to oneself. The three are restatements of the same thing.

[14. Answers to possible objections]
- Objection 1: the relation between life and mind/consciousness, and the ecological character of the mind, look necessary on Earth but may be contingent; mind without life may be possible. Artificial life research explores the nature of life not limited to what was born on Earth. His reply: think and try building as much as you like; he does not deny the results of metaphysical thinking, thought experiments and simulations. But he wants to keep carefully looking at life that has existed, exists and will exist on Earth, and at relations between life and environment and among living things. Losing that viewpoint and concentrating only on "life as it could be" seems to him too one-sided a metaphysics.
- Objection 2: plant perception and behavior are genetically programmed fixed response rules or, however complex, sophisticated automatic reflexes, different in kind from those of humans and animals with nervous systems (he also recalled a contrast in Dennett's book involving competence). He thinks this will be overturned empirically; the flexibility, plasticity and learning of plants and other nerveless organisms, including their limits, will be increasingly clarified. Behavioral research on plants keeps increasing.
- Objection 3 (probably the most damaging): plant perception is unconscious, not conscious; consciousness without phenomenality is not consciousness. The slide first concedes that this is so. The day before, the view that consciousness disappears during sleep came up, but on his view one would have to say consciousness is still present: a sleeping person slapping and scratching a mosquito bite would count as conscious in that sense.
- Rather, the view that treats conscious and unconscious as mutually exclusive and binary is biased. Even in humans, aren't the feet "aware" of the floor when walking? This also overlaps with the everyday use of the word "conscious".
- Objection 4: this concerns only plant access consciousness (cognition), not phenomenal consciousness; plants have no phenomenal consciousness. He should really reread and criticize Block's paper, but 30 years have passed since access and phenomenal consciousness were distinguished; it is time to reconsider.
- Can there be cognition without consciousness? This touches on the possibility of zombies, which also came up the day before, and he is negative about the possibility of zombies.
- Objection 5: anthropomorphism and zoomorphism. Applying psychological concepts unique to humans and animals to plants hides the true uniqueness of plant life; the French philosopher Burgat ("What Is a Plant?") makes this criticism.
- He would turn it around: this very view is a rigid anthropomorphism unable to relativize human-centered psychological concepts. Let us recognize the diversity of mind and consciousness. He would answer by asking why a logic that works for digestion does not work for mental workings.
- He closed by saying that some questions cannot be answered cleanly as black or white, so parts may sound inconclusive.

[15. Q&A]
- A participant, saying they too had thought consciousness was impossible without nerves and had discriminated against plants, asked whether AI would be conscious if it overcame the three points he raised; sensing the environment and changing itself seems already possible or nearly so for AI. Someya said that if these are taken as criteria for mind and consciousness, he would in principle answer yes, though there is plenty to criticize. If one accepts the argument about possible life and possible consciousness - that Earth is not the only environment - he cannot currently find a decisive judgment against saying "this is AI". Taking in the natural environment as information with cameras and learning and choosing could also be done with sensors. But what he currently feels as life involves at least metabolism - taking in energy sources from the surroundings to maintain oneself, digesting, absorbing and excreting - as part of the connections in natural cycles; including that in the definition of life makes it still difficult. AI might acquire that, and even if not, the argument continues that if it could behave so in possible environments other than Earth, it might be called life.
- Someya added that he does not much like metaphysical arguments. Thought experiments can advance some things, but for the argument to be meaningful, one must consider possibilities while incorporating what empirical science has actually found. Creating possible worlds without any constraint is, he thinks, where philosophy goes a bit too far (he cited the example of a possibility very remote from reality, such as water freezing at 100 degrees).
- A participant pointed out that his dissatisfactions with AI and robots might have held in 2025, but with the trend since 2026 from single agents to multi-agent systems, they may hardly hold within a year or two; researchers around the world, including Ikegami, are trying to build self-sustaining systems that secure their own resources. Asked what he thought, Someya said that if that happens, philosophers who want to join seriously in the debate over whether it has life will have to get inside it. Considering the rest of his life he does not know how far he will go, but he hopes young, capable philosophers with interest will cut into that debate, and he will make efforts too.
- A participant asked whether, if the scope is extended, it should go beyond life to what is usually called inorganic, especially soil, the medium that grows plants: perhaps soil is alive too, or soil is what is alive; there is also the idea that all living things are born from soil and return to it. The questioner said they themselves treat this as "life" in a different sense from what they mean by life, consciousness or mind, but if Someya holds that all life has mind and consciousness in the same sense, shouldn't he say that soil too has mind and consciousness? Someya said he had not prepared for this but did not think he could answer yes. He thinks it connects to a uniquely Japanese idea, extending even to plants, trees and the land, that life dwells in all things, but he would like to prepare his words further.
- A participant asked how plant consciousness, if assumed, differs from human or animal consciousness, for example in self-identity: humans, at least modern people, feel self-identity strongly, but trees often grow many stems from the same roots, so it is unclear whether they are one or many. Someya said he had no positive answer, but introduced the field of "plant philosophy", which took shape around the same time as plant neurobiology: Michael Marder, Emanuele Coccia and others argue actively that by looking at plant life we can deconstruct concepts that Western philosophy has used to define humans essentially, such as individuality and identity. He is not much committed himself but hopes they go ahead; identity itself becomes diverse, and in widening our thinking this goes in the same direction as the discussion of consciousness and mind.
- A participant noted that human nerve conduction is very fast, e.g., saltatory conduction, whereas plants use very slow calcium-ion signals, and asked about time: plants cannot avoid having leaves eaten but respond by secreting toxins afterwards; if humans feel pain for a certain time, does a tree living thousands of years suffer for a very long time? What about the temporal aspect of experience? Someya said the time scales of humans and animals versus plants differ considerably, and that difference conversely makes plant behavior and perception hard to see; strategies for dealing with enemies also differ. He thinks there is ample room to consider the differences among forms of life as differences in the time scales on which they live.
- A participant who has long debated with him said what still does not convince them is the combination problem: if cells have cognition, why are we, multicellular bodies, a single consciousness? Someya said he has no positive answer yet, but borrowing the CPC (collective predictive coding) from Suzuki's talk, one could argue that even if individual cells are not conscious, one consciousness emerges when they form a collective. Proponents of the cellular consciousness hypothesis are also starting to propose that consciousness integrates between cells in multicellularity, and to discuss how, before neurons appeared, electrical mechanisms let cells act in a coordinated way that contributes to the individual's survival, and how when this breaks down, cells such as cancer cells proliferate regardless of their surroundings.
- Someya further said that the premise of "one consciousness per person" may also need more thought. When walking, the whole body including hands, feet and neck moves in an integrated way, but if each part - for example the cells of the feet sensing the ground - is considered, what makes us think of a single consciousness may actually be a rather special mode of organizing consciousness. The questioner replied that cognition happening in many places is fine; it always comes down to a difference in terminology between the questioner, who wants to keep "consciousness" in reserve, and Someya, who wants to take it down to lower levels as a synonym for cognition. The discussion ended there due to time.
