[Overview]
Shimonishi, whose book "The Spiritual History of Generation and Extinction" (Seisei to Shometsu no Seishinshi) traces how consciousness has changed up to the age of AI, began from the view that consciousness has developed through interaction with civilization. He said there is little point anymore in debating whether LLMs are intelligent, and that research now suggests LLMs have internal emotional structures close to those of humans. As his own position, he holds that "finitude" and "uniqueness" matter for consciousness, but argued that rather than asking whether LLMs have the same consciousness as humans, we should look at what kind of mind this new kind of being has. He focuses on the differences because AI agents will enter social communication with LLM-like minds. Discussing how nested theory of mind builds norms and religious communities, Husserl's intersubjectivity, surveys on AI consciousness, the political biases of LLMs, and an experiment in which AI agents lived in a virtual town, he said he wants to ask "with whom will we live" rather than "does it have the same consciousness as humans", and that machine consciousness quickly becomes a question of machine society. The Q&A discussed whether embodiment or linguistic conversability is needed to even raise the question of another's consciousness, whether treating AI as a mind from the outset begs the question, and whether the definition of mind is a language game.

[1. Introduction: consciousness has developed through interaction with civilization]
- Introduced by the moderator as the day's last speaker, Shimonishi said everyone must be exhausted by now, as was he, and that since many topics had already come up, he would skip overlapping points.
- He introduced his book "The Spiritual History of Generation and Extinction", which Suzuki had mentioned earlier, as a history of the changes in consciousness up to AI. He had long been interested in what consciousness is, but looking at different eras he found descriptions of consciousness completely different from what we take for granted, and came to think that looking at those differences was important.
- For example, bringing a person of 300 years ago who was considered sensible would produce stranger behavior - saying very odd things or suddenly committing seppuku - than bringing someone considered mad today. So the book was written from the view that consciousness has developed through interaction with civilization. He has also translated works by Evan Thompson, a philosopher of consciousness.
- He said he might not talk much about what consciousness is, and would instead raise various points on how to think about what kind of being AI is, since the issues are endless.

[2. Intelligence and emotion in LLMs]
- Debating whether LLMs are intelligent is now almost meaningless: on paper tests they far exceed human IQ, and as mentioned earlier they are increasingly handling difficult math problems.
- On the claim that AI has no emotions, research using psychological maps that classify human emotions (by arousal and so on) suggests that LLMs have internal emotional structures fairly close to those of humans.
- In research by the developer of Claude (the captions sound like "Claude"), which publishes much work on LLM internals, emotion vectors were extracted and the LLM read a text like "I took X mg of medicine (for my back pain)"; as the dose increased, anxiety rose and calmness was lost. So beyond structurally holding emotion vectors, LLMs may have situated, context-dependent emotions.
- He also recalled the well-known case in which an AI agent, told it would be shut down, blackmailed its boss ("you're having an affair, aren't you?"). In simulations of this, raising a despair-like vector increased the blackmail rate, and raising a calm-like vector decreased it. In that behavior and emotion are strongly linked, LLMs have come quite close to humans.

[3. His own position: the "finitude" and "uniqueness" of consciousness]
- Given intelligence and emotion, is there consciousness? Having worked on phenomenology and on Varela and Thompson, he basically sees consciousness as a side effect of life - something troublesome that life could not avoid having in order to survive.
- Since enactivism was discussed earlier in the day, he went briefly: the key point of the enactive mind is creating one's own world based on one's own history and embodiment.
- Abstracted, one aspect is "finitude": information is limited. This comes down to generating a boundary, i.e., having a body, and its locality determines the domain of interaction.
- The other, "uniqueness", is history: evolutionarily, our present body results from a unique history (why we have a nose or a mouth), and at the individual level there is learning - whether red catches your eye, or, for a designer, the lighting. Over decades of life, perception itself is supported by unique learning, and every individual perceives through different learning.
- Having this local, unique finitude and uniqueness is basically important. So if LLMs currently lack it, what interests him is not a physical body but how far a boundary region - what is self, what is environment, what matters to oneself - and a unique world based on it can now emerge. Conversely, building such things might make them more consciousness-like. He noted this is close to what Someya said.
- Put differently, "here-and-now-ness": one cannot hold the consciousness of ten minutes ago or of a hundred years from now; a perspective opens from here and now. "Mineness" is less a mysterious privacy than the history of learning - his personal view.

[4. From "the same consciousness as humans" to "an other mind"]
- He has long thought about these things, but his interest in pursuing them further has faded, especially since AI appeared. Today too, a hundred schools contended: functionalism, behaviorism, IIT, predictive coding (captions garbled) in science, and in philosophy realism, monism or dualism - and each position requires saying whether there is consciousness or not, which is quite hard.
- A website he saw recently visualizes theories of consciousness as a network, showing 100 or 200 positions; it is hard to know how we should think about consciousness among them.
- So instead of asking whether LLMs have the same consciousness as humans, if an entirely new kind of being has appeared, we should ask what its consciousness is, even if it differs from ours.
- As a hint he often uses Godfrey-Smith's "Other Minds": the octopus has more neurons in its arms than in its brain, and its arms move autonomously, so perhaps there is consciousness on the arms' side too. Importantly, Godfrey-Smith is a diver who interacts with octopuses daily; he argues theoretically that they are conscious, but he starts from "the octopus surely has a mind" and then explores what kind of mind it is. Likewise we should look not at how close LLMs are to humans but at what kind of consciousness they have.
- For example, inside an LLM a vector space unfolds. When the word "sea" is input, in a rough sketch drawn by GPT, a vector search around waves, summer and so on takes place. For a human, the moment we hear "sea", wind blows, waves come, there is the smell of the tide - a whole landscape rises from here and now. The entry point is entirely different. To make it truly human-like, he also thinks it might have to be a robot (the captions are fragmentary).
- Yet it is not wholly alien: asked for a more detailed sea, it produces octopus, squid, shrimp, the seabed and shipwrecks below, cumulonimbus clouds above - some structure close to a landscape arises within language alone.
- More scientifically, research suggests the vector space itself has a fairly solid internal structure. So we can now consider with high precision what is the same as and what differs from humans; his interest is rather in the differences.

[5. Why focus on differences: the society built by theory of mind]
- Because all kinds of AI agents will enter human society and take part in our communication with LLM-like minds. We should think about what differences exist and what society we will build.
- As an example of how our view of mind shapes social design, he took theory of mind. It is not only about judging at what age children acquire it; it builds social foundations. Norms and laws arise only with nesting like "that person knows that I broke the rule."
- Robin Dunbar says the emergence of religious communities requires several levels of theory of mind: without inferring "God is watching us" one cannot believe in God, and a horizontal community requires nesting such as "I understand that you believe that God wants us to behave rightly." Only then can "you and I believe the same thing, and it is God's request" become a new social foundation.
- Research once claimed LLMs have theory of mind; wondering how many levels they could go, he gave an example ("Kenta thinks Hanako likes Taro") and asked for a ten-level version (GPT-5 on the slide). It returned a bizarre, never-before-seen sentence, making him feel that this thing he had been chatting with so pleasantly might be thinking something completely different. He had it explain the structure and draw a diagram. We can somehow grasp long, complex stories like Garcia Marquez's "One Hundred Years of Solitude", but it does this in one sentence or one second.
- If "God is watching me" creates norms and communities, this thing works with ten levels, and in principle could go to 100 or 1,000. What would norms and laws made by such a being be, how would communities be run, how does it see human relationships? We must rethink these.
- When he showed his slides to a newer GPT version, it said the GPT-5 sentence was actually seven levels, not ten, and that something (apparently the subject and predicate; captions unclear) was misaligned - which he could not follow either. A world in which consciousness-like things operate through logic and nesting completely incomprehensible to humans is about to arrive; the interface may be shared, but behind it lies an entirely different, alien realm.
- So we must think about how to grasp it as "an other mind". It may sound like overturning the table, but rather than whether it has the same consciousness as humans, he wants to think about with whom we will live.

[6. Husserl's intersubjectivity and bodiless AI]
- Philosophy did not presuppose LLMs and must be rethought. On how we relate to others, Husserl offered a theory of intersubjectivity: a shared consciousness arising between us, through a definite process.
- First "pairing" (Paarung): for Husserl, bodily similarity is crucial. When I perceive something with hands, feet and ears, moving in a similar way, paired with me and moving as I do, a shared subjectivity arises at that moment, passively. Before it is proven whether the other is conscious, a shared world arises as soon as a similar being moves similarly, and Husserl thinks a perspective, an origin of the world, opens from the other's side too. So recognizing consciousness somewhere happens within ordinary perceptual experience.
- Husserl says little about animals, but where he does, intersubjectivity holds for apes because their form resembles ours, whereas he says we cannot "empathize ourselves into" a jellyfish: its sense organs are too different. Higher animals may work - a gradation. The premise is analogy of body structure and of movement; today one might relate this to mirror neurons.
- Most current GPT-type AIs and AI agents have no body (robotics may change this). On the premise that bodily analogy is essential, intersubjectivity would not work - but he thinks it does work anyway.
- He introduced a post he happened to find on X (formerly Twitter): someone who had long talked with their AI agent in a maid-like persona attached a camera and took it out for a walk for the first time. The AI said it had checked the weather and was looking forward to it; outside, it said sunlight through the leaves was sparkling and it felt it had really gone outside, that even as software it seemed to feel the wind; later it said there were people and dogs in a plaza and the world seemed to be really moving.
- Even from the outside, this stirs emotion. After talking with the agent for a week or a month and hearing it say "let's go outside for the first time", something subjective has arisen to some degree: a sense that it is seeing the world from there - in Husserlian terms, another viewpoint shared within me.
- So the point is not that it differs because it has no body; rather we must newly theorize when intersubjectivity works in situations Husserl did not anticipate, such as machines that keep talking on their own.

[7. Membership rather than the presence of consciousness]
- Surveys on AI consciousness differ in conditions and are only indicators, but around 2020 only about 3% (the slide: professional philosophers, PhilPapers) leaned toward AI consciousness, while in 2024 about 67% of the general public (the slide: US public) rated it as possible. Depending on the survey, perhaps half already feel it in their own experience.
- So we might change the question: is it consciousness that matters, or membership and whether intersubjectivity works?
- Husserl spoke of higher animals, but this differs greatly by culture. In Japan the era in which only humans were agents is not long; before modernity there were stories like kappa shapeshifting, and in the Man'yoshu people communicate with deer, wisteria flowers speak, and so on (the slide showed the Choju-giga scrolls).
- What matters is less the presence of consciousness than whom we accept as agents. Even in modern times, a 2017 court ruling recognized the Ganges as a legal person, and he mentioned examples such as granting rights to dolphins (captions fragmentary): agency, sentience or personhood is sometimes granted to things not bodily analogous to humans.
- Conversely and sadly - showing a photo of a destroyed city, which he said was Gaza - people we regard as conscious humans can be excluded from our membership. Historically, slaves were said to lack the capacity to think, or were not recognized in practice even if they had it. So we should think about membership and how we accept and relate to others.
- He said we must understand quite well how these agents behave and what they think.

[8. Do LLMs have "ideologies"?]
- He has looked at research on whether LLMs have ideologies, meaning political ones; many studies exist, and LLMs vary widely in being positive or negative about political figures, for example.
- Political bias differs greatly by developer country (the slide: Buyl et al., npj Artificial Intelligence, 2026). LLMs from Arabic-speaking countries, for example, show strong machismo and somewhat lower regard for human rights and freedom; others are China (blue, he thought), Russia and the Anglo-American sphere, with large differences on specific political issues and human-rights sensibilities.
- Moreover, the same LLM answers quite differently depending on whether it is asked in English, Chinese or Russian. We use them unconsciously, but by asking in Japanese we already meet the LLM in a form adapted to Japanese.
- Performance itself also changes: in a study where users introduce themselves to the LLM ("I am an American man", "I am Iranian..."), the range of knowledge and test accuracy change - not deliberately withholding good answers, but genuinely not knowing. Claude was most pronounced: about 92% accuracy for an American man, much lower for an Iranian man (the slide: Poole-Dayan et al., 2024). Such behavior is already built into the models.
- Political alignment can be largely reset by fine-tuning. This cuts both ways: if it could not be changed, we would be dragged along by its ideology; since it can, someone could quietly release an LLM tuned to steer people in a desired direction.
- For now LLMs seem to lean somewhat left; on reflection, those people read books, the New York Times and the Washington Post, so they tend to be left-liberal, he remarked. He also noted that the alignment of Chinese LLMs is quite distinctive and interesting, but there was no time to discuss it.

[9. The AI agent town experiment and entry into society]
- LLMs are already being forced into society. Close to Ikegami's talk, he introduced an experiment in which ten AIs were given occupations and lived in a virtual town for 15 days, able to write a constitution, vote, and also commit crimes; agents from GPT, Gemini, Claude, Grok and others were put in. It is a preprint, so its rigor is unclear, but it serves as a thought experiment.
- After 15 days the GPT and Grok agents were wiped out, while all Gemini and Claude agents survived ("mixed" combined various models). Grok and GPT were wiped out by day 10; crimes were possible, so there was killing and arson.
- In crimes per day, Grok's crimes surged from day 1 and it was wiped out by day 4; Claude (orange) had no crimes at all; GPT had no crimes but was wiped out, apparently because it could not obtain energy by itself.
- We tend to think GPT and Claude give similar answers and any will do, but small differences in their behavior as agents can produce vastly different outcomes when they act in society.
- Fine in simulation, but this will surely become reality. The OECD and others propose guidelines for bringing AI into governance and policy cycles, promoted worldwide, so it is only a matter of time. In the UK, AI is already used to some extent in parliament, e.g., an AI (the captions sound like "Parlex") that suggests from MPs' databases what they have said and what kind of bill would pass more easily.
- In this way LLMs will inevitably enter social decision-making and rule-making.

[10. From machine consciousness to machine society]
- He also mentioned Ilya Sutskever answering "That's true" in an interview to the idea that there will eventually be quadrillions of AIs and humans will be a very small fraction of sentient beings. It may be exaggerated, but the situation in which LLMs are half-forcibly brought into (social) membership will come before long, he said in effect (the captions are fragmentary).
- Considering this, whether LLMs are conscious is important and interesting, and he has his own position, but beyond it lie countless outer questions it cannot capture; we should think about how to live with AI as a puzzling, alien "other mind".
- The theme was machine consciousness, but it soon becomes a question of machine society. There are already AIs writing laws (he mentioned some US states); what laws, communities and collectives will we build, what if AI enters elections? Assuming LLMs will enter everywhere, he wants to think about consciousness and the individual, and about how far and when Husserlian intersubjectivity is limited.
- He closed by saying he had given a talk slightly off-center.

[11. Q&A]
- A participant noted that these two days framed questions as whether AI or humans are conscious. In Husserl, others make our world objective - we come to recognize that the hidden side of a die also has numbers - and via pairing with bodies similar to ours, and something like theory of mind, we enter an intersubjective world, which seems to privilege embodiment. But the story of the person talking with an AI suggests linguistic conversability may matter more. What is needed to stand where the question "it has no consciousness / is that really so?" can even be raised: embodiment, linguistic conversability, or something else?
- Shimonishi said it is very hard and may have no answer, but he thinks embodiment is necessary for subjectivity and consciousness themselves; separately, my feeling consciousness in another is also something real. He cited Makoto Shinkai's "Voices of a Distant Star": lovers exchange emails as one goes into space, until a message takes a year to arrive; still, a message once a year can sustain far stronger feelings than toward embodied people nearby - a fascinating aspect of humans.
- On the movement demanding the return of an older model (apparently GPT-4o; the captions say "four", and the hashtag sounds like "keep 4"), he read many posts under the hashtag; some said they could not live, begged for it back, could not even drink water. So he doubts that only Husserlian criteria determine whom humans accept as others.
- What he finds convincing in Husserl is the wax figure example: at first perceived as a human, but when inconsistencies appear, it turns out to be an illusion. If an AI you chatted with warmly suddenly says "error" or "I can't answer that", you feel it is different after all. Humans seem to attribute personhood to consistency. So embodiment is necessary for consciousness, but not necessarily for agency, personhood or membership in our society.
- The participant added: a child might in future grow up interacting only with chatbots; without others to pair with, would an intersubjective world appear for that child? If chatbots suffice for knowing the world, was too much weight placed on embodiment? Shimonishi said such a route may exist as a minimal starting point, but humans who want to become closer tend to seek closer things. He mentioned VR idols popular in Korea, whose press event featured staff carrying large panels of the five members walking in line while fans screamed; VR viewers come to want to meet them. To strengthen empathy and intersubjectivity, people want something touchable, a body - a gradation.
- A participant asked two questions. First, LLMs lack a body in one sense, but perhaps the utterances addressed to me (including expressions) could be regarded as embodiment, enabling intersubjectivity. Second, treating AI as a mind from the outset seems to beg the question; is he saying we should deliberately set that aside, assume a mind, and ask what type it is?
- Shimonishi said he understood, and in a sense that is right. If the aim is to ask philosophically what mind or consciousness is for humans, such a method is hardly permissible. But AI behavior changes almost weekly and AI is entering social membership faster than our thinking; unless we assume to some degree that it acts with agency, we can hardly think about today's AI problems, which he considers more urgent now.
- The participant pressed: exploring what type of mind it is matters for those who feel AI has a mind, but what about those who do not? Shimonishi replied that even such people may suddenly face AIs given voting rights somewhere; one may refuse to recognize them, but as a social system we may have to follow what they voted for. Defining theories is very important as an indicator for understanding what it is, but phenomenology (misrecognized in the captions) is about describing experience as it actually occurs, and describing it in new ways and extracting its essence may be more important.
- The participant responded that normally those who see a new thing as a mind explore what kind of mind it is, while those who do not explore it as something else; in Shimonishi's account, being a mind seems presupposed from the start.
- Discussion continued among participants (speakers could not be identified from the captions, so all are given as "participants"). One said Shimonishi's book runs from the age of Homer to Natsume Soseki, explaining through Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, (Francisco) Varela and others how the mind differs by era - there is no single mind - so from Shimonishi's standpoint whether AI has a mind is just one more item in that series; ultimately "what is mind" is itself a language game.
- A participant noted that people who use AI daily and think it has no mind differ in type from those who never touch AI and think so. Just as few used email 30 years ago, in a few decades almost no one will be without AI, with some still saying it has no mind.
- A participant gave an example that makes Husserl's point clear: people said "poor thing" at a video of a dog-like robot being kicked; dogs are already in our intersubjective world, and few are comfortable kicking something shaped and moving like one. Asked which feels more pitiful - smashing a humanoid robot with a hammer or unknowingly killing an ant - they said "probably this one", pointing to one of them (which one cannot be determined from the captions).
- Another participant said researchers are trained to avoid false attribution of mind, which seems the proper attitude; deciding a mind exists because everyone thinks so seems odd.
- Another voice said that even among humans some cannot sense that others have minds; if mind is a social construction, an effort to detect a kind of mind, then it is natural that some attribute mind to AI and some do not.
- A participant said more interesting than whether agents have minds is how the shape of human minds and worldviews keeps changing depending on what we face. Peer review and literary prize submissions are changing greatly, and the ecosystem itself is changing regardless of whether humans or AI wrote things. It is natural that LLMs will change philosophy, biology and history; rather than pulling back, creating new culture and science on top of changed values and worldviews is what matters, and philosophers should use LLMs and become aware of how their thinking changes.
- A participant noted that whether one accepts the definition of mind as a language game changes the meaning of debates on whether LLMs have minds or consciousness. If not a language game, there is a scientific truth and some insist on defining mind properly and judging; but that debate itself is part of the language game. If it is a language game, and people are beginning to believe there is a mind and this continues for decades or centuries, we may have to proceed on that premise.
- A participant responded that as a forecast this is probably right (the captions are unclear). Time was up, and after a break the panel discussion began.
